WORKSHOP 2 · ARTIFACT 2

Adaptive Learning in Semiconductor Manufacturing

Leadership Discussion: Adaptive Learning and Change Management

This artifact connects adaptive learning and change leadership with my professional experience as a Failure Analysis Engineer in the semiconductor industry. It highlights how engineers and leaders can use feedback, failure data, teamwork, and continuous improvement to respond to changing technical conditions.

Objective

To demonstrate how adaptive learning supports successful change by helping people evaluate new information, adjust problem-solving methods, and improve processes as technology and workplace needs evolve.

Professional Experience

In my professional experience as a Failure Analysis Engineer in the semiconductor industry, I understand the importance of continuous learning and adaptation to change. My responsibilities include investigating hardware failures in PCBAs and server systems, analyzing test results, identifying root causes, and collaborating with engineering, quality, and production teams to implement solutions. Monitoring test failure trends, improving troubleshooting procedures, and implementing corrective actions can help prevent the same problems from happening again.

When a new product is introduced into production, unexpected hardware failures or testing issues may occur. Instead of following the same troubleshooting approach every time, engineers need to analyze new failure data, learn from previous issues, collect feedback from technicians, and adjust their methods. Updating troubleshooting guidelines and sharing technical knowledge with team members can improve problem-solving and production quality.

AI-Generated Change Scenario

The hypothetical scenario considers a manufacturing company introducing an AI and machine learning system to predict equipment failures and identify possible production quality issues. At first, employees may find it difficult to trust the system because they are more familiar with traditional testing and troubleshooting methods. The system may also produce inaccurate predictions during its early stages.

An adaptive leader would introduce the system gradually, provide employee training, collect feedback, and monitor its performance. The leader would work with the technical team to review results, improve the model, and make necessary changes to the implementation process. This approach gives employees time to understand the new technology while allowing the organization to learn from early results.

Leadership Takeaways

  • Use evidence: Review test results, failure trends, and feedback before deciding what needs to change.
  • Support people: Provide training and encourage employees to share concerns and observations.
  • Adjust the approach: Treat early problems as information that can guide improvements to the technology and the implementation plan.
  • Keep human judgment involved: Check AI predictions and technical recommendations instead of accepting them without verification.

Skills Demonstrated

Root-cause analysis Problem-solving Continuous improvement Cross-functional teamwork Adaptability Responsible AI awareness

What I Learned

This activity helped me understand that successful leadership is not only about introducing new technology. Leaders also need to understand how people respond to change, listen to feedback, and make adjustments based on actual results. In failure analysis, the same mindset is useful because each issue can provide information that improves future troubleshooting and production quality.

WORKSHOP 3 · ARTIFACT 3

Machine Learning Training Methods — Learning Partner Activity

Skills Demonstrated: Understanding foundational machine learning approaches, explaining model-training concepts, and connecting AI/ML ideas to engineering applications.

This learning activity uses an AI learning partner to explore seven introductory questions about how machine learning models are trained. The goal is to understand the basic ideas and recognize when different approaches may be useful, rather than study advanced mathematics or programming.

Learning Objectives

Build a foundation in supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning; understand the role of algorithms, data, iteration, and evaluation; and connect these ideas to practical engineering examples.

Seven Guiding Questions and Learning Notes

  1. How does a supervised learning model learn from training data?
    A supervised model learns from examples that include inputs and correct answers, called labels. It compares predictions with the labels and adjusts its internal settings to reduce errors. For example, it can learn from PCBA test measurements paired with known Pass or Fail results.
  2. What is the main approach used to train models in unsupervised learning?
    Unsupervised learning uses data without supplied answer labels. The model looks for patterns, similarities, groups, or unusual observations. For example, it could group PCBA test records with similar measurement patterns for further engineering investigation.
  3. In reinforcement learning, how does an agent learn which actions are most useful?
    An agent interacts with an environment, tries actions, and receives rewards or penalties. It uses this feedback to learn a strategy that aims to earn more reward over time. The reward must be designed carefully so the agent learns behavior that matches the real goal.
  4. Why are algorithms important in training machine learning models?
    An algorithm provides the procedure a model uses to learn from data. It guides how patterns are identified and how model parameters are adjusted. Different algorithms have different strengths, limitations, and resource requirements.
  5. What are the basic steps involved in training a machine learning model?
    A common workflow is to define the problem, collect and prepare data, select features and a model, separate training and evaluation data, train, evaluate, make improvements, and monitor the model after deployment. The exact workflow depends on the project.
  6. How does repetition, or iterating over data, help train a model?
    Repeated training gives a model more opportunities to adjust its parameters based on errors or feedback. One complete pass through the training dataset is often called an epoch. Training needs monitoring because too little or too much training can both cause problems.
  7. What role do examples, or data, play in training a machine learning model?
    Data provides the examples and experience from which a model learns. Relevant, varied, and representative data supports better learning. Poor-quality, incomplete, biased, or outdated data can produce unreliable results.

Important Difference Among the Three Main Learning Approaches

  • Supervised learning: learns from examples with known labels or answers.
  • Unsupervised learning: finds patterns in data without supplied labels.
  • Reinforcement learning: learns actions through interaction and reward-based feedback.

Connection to Semiconductor Engineering

These approaches can be related to semiconductor manufacturing and failure analysis. Supervised learning could use historical test measurements with Pass/Fail labels to estimate the result for a new board. Unsupervised learning could identify groups of similar failure patterns when labels are unavailable. Reinforcement learning could be explored in a carefully controlled simulation to study process decisions. These are examples of possible applications, not claims that such models have been deployed in my workplace.

Learning Recap

  • Models learn from data, but the type of feedback depends on the learning approach.
  • Supervised learning uses labeled examples, while unsupervised learning searches for patterns without labels.
  • Reinforcement learning uses actions and reward-based feedback.
  • Algorithms guide the learning process, and repeated training updates model parameters.
  • Data quality and separate evaluation on unseen examples are important for judging reliability.

Further question: How can an engineer decide whether a supervised or unsupervised approach is more appropriate when investigating a new type of hardware failure?

Machine learning fundamentals Critical thinking Data understanding Model evaluation Engineering application

Key Takeaway: Machine learning models learn from examples, patterns, or feedback. Selecting an approach depends on the problem, the available data, and how success will be evaluated.

WORKSHOP 4 · ARTIFACT 4

Data Challenge Scenarios — Building Reliable and Responsible ML Systems

Skills Demonstrated: Data quality assessment, model evaluation, fairness analysis, privacy-aware machine learning, and responsible AI decision-making.

This artifact presents my responses to three practical scenarios involving machine learning systems. Each scenario focuses on a challenge that can affect whether an AI application is reliable, fair, secure, and appropriate for real-world use. I selected this activity because it shows that developing a useful model involves more than choosing an algorithm or having a large dataset.

Scenario 1 — Data Quality and Cleaning

A customer-review company is preparing a sentiment analysis model using 800,000 reviews. The dataset contains duplicates, missing information, inconsistent formats, multiple languages, spam, and labels that are partly based on star ratings.

My approach is to review the dataset before training and preserve a backup of the original information. I would identify duplicate records, handle missing values, correct inconsistent formats, and investigate possible spam. I would also check whether sentiment labels accurately represent the review text, because a five-star rating does not always mean that every part of a review is positive. I would avoid automatically removing different languages, emojis, or unusual expressions because they may contain useful information.

Important evaluation point: Duplicate or highly similar reviews should not be split across training and test sets. Otherwise, the model may appear more accurate because it has already encountered similar examples. I would compare performance before and after cleaning and keep related records in the same data split.

Scenario 2 — Bias, Distribution Shift, and Responsible Scaling

A resume-screening model was trained on ten years of hiring data from one company. The company plans to use it in another country and increase application volume by ten times. Historical hiring decisions may contain unfair patterns, and applicants in a new country may have different qualifications, resume formats, languages, and professional experiences.

Before expansion, I would test the model using representative data from the new country and compare its results across applicant groups. I would examine false positives and false negatives, including how often qualified applicants are incorrectly rejected, instead of relying only on overall accuracy. I would recommend a small pilot, continued monitoring, and human review for important hiring decisions.

Important evaluation point: Strong performance on historical data does not guarantee fair or accurate results in a new population. The organization should check for differences in error rates across groups and investigate any patterns that suggest unequal treatment.

Scenario 3 — Data Sharing, Privacy, Security, and Governance

Two hospitals want to improve a diagnostic machine-learning model but do not want to transfer their original patient records to one central location. I identified federated learning as one possible approach. Each hospital can keep its records within its own system while participating in a shared model-training process.

Federated learning does not automatically guarantee complete privacy. The hospitals should also consider encryption, secure access controls, authentication, monitoring, secure aggregation, and clear rules for data use and retention. They should check that their data formats and diagnostic labels are compatible, then evaluate the model using data from both organizations.

Important evaluation point: Information may still be exposed through model updates or exchanged information, and a participating system could be compromised. Privacy protection therefore requires both technical safeguards and organizational responsibility.

Key Lessons Across the Three Scenarios

  • Data quality affects model quality: Duplicates, missing values, inaccurate labels, and inconsistent formats can distort training and evaluation.
  • Fairness requires more than overall accuracy: Models should be evaluated across relevant groups, especially when they are used in a new population or setting.
  • Privacy is a continuing responsibility: Federated learning can reduce the need to centralize raw data, but additional security and governance controls remain necessary.
  • Evaluation must continue after deployment: Models need monitoring because data, users, and operating conditions can change.
  • Human oversight matters: High-impact decisions should include review, accountability, and a way to investigate questionable outcomes.

Professional Relevance

These ideas connect with engineering and semiconductor manufacturing, where data quality, changing operating conditions, and reliable evaluation matter when analyzing test results and failure trends. For example, inaccurate or duplicated test records could affect an analysis of recurring failures. A model developed using one product or production condition may also need additional evaluation before being applied to a different product or environment. The same disciplined approach applies: understand the data, test assumptions, examine errors, protect information, and monitor results.
